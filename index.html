Sequence step 01 · Baseline

Your family benchmark is context, not a verdict.

Net worth is what you own minus what you owe. The age-band median is a national midpoint — orientation, not a grade.

Why this step?
Median beats average here

A small number of very wealthy households pull averages upward. The median is the household in the middle: half are above it and half are below.

Sequence step 02 · Monthly cash flow

Map the bills before moving money.

Surplus is take-home minus every expense: rent, food, utilities, transport, subscriptions, debt minimums. Then flag the costs you must keep paying — those set your savings targets.

Why this step?
Debt does not disqualify you

Keep every required minimum payment current. Sequence builds a cash cushion before directing extra money to debt, so the next surprise does not have to go straight back on a card.

1
Find take-home income

Use the amount that actually reaches checking after taxes and paycheck deductions.

2
Total every monthly expense

Include minimum debt payments. For irregular bills, use a monthly average.

3
Mark the essentials

Housing, basic food, utilities, transportation, insurance, and required debt payments usually belong here.

Sequence step 03 · Checking hub

Make checking your bill-paying hub.

Stop late fees and overdrafts. Then keep 1–2 months of expenses in checking.

Why this step?
Two different credit-card paths

If you do not carry a balance, set auto-pay to the full statement balance. If you already carry debt, stop adding new purchases when possible, automate at least the required minimum, and build your cash cushion before making extra payoff payments.

1
Choose one checking hub

Use the account where income lands and recurring bills are easiest to see.

2
Set the buffer target

Start with one month of essential expenses—including minimum debt payments; use two when income or bill timing is less predictable.

3
Turn on bill auto-pay

Schedule rent, utilities, subscriptions, and minimum debt payments from the hub.

4
Use credit deliberately

If you pay in full, put normal daily spending on one primary card for rewards and fraud protection, then auto-pay the full statement balance. Keeping reported utilization below the framework’s 10–15% guardrail may help your credit profile, but no utilization level guarantees a 740+ score.

Sequence step 04 · Employer benefits

Capture the full employer match.

If your job matches retirement contributions, grab the full match — even while building savings or carrying debt.

Why this step?
What “match” means

Your employer adds money when you contribute under the plan’s rules. “100% match up to 4%” usually means you contribute 4% of pay and the employer adds another 4%. Vesting rules determine when that employer money fully belongs to you.

1
Open the benefits portal

Look for retirement, 401(k), contribution rate, employer match, and vesting.

2
Find the match cap

Write down the contribution percentage needed to receive every matching dollar.

3
Set that exact percentage

If the full match requires 4% or 6%, choose that rate—not the employer’s default. The election then deducts automatically from each paycheck. Traditional 401(k) contributions are generally pre-tax; Roth 401(k) contributions are after-tax.

4
Choose a straightforward fund

A low-cost target-date fund or broad-market index fund can be a simple starting point. Verify the deduction and match on your next paystub.

Check your match status

Your benefits portal or plan document will show the maximum match and vesting rules.

Sequence step 05 · Emergency reserve

Protect the plan with an emergency reserve.

Emergency cash sits in a high-yield savings account: separate, accessible, earning interest. Target 3–6 months of essentials before extra debt payoff.

Why this step?
HYSA, in plain English

It is a savings account, not an investment account: the balance does not rise and fall with the stock market. Look for FDIC insurance, no monthly maintenance fee, easy transfers, and a competitive rate. The framework’s 4–5% is only an illustration; rates change.

1
Choose a separate HYSA

Marcus, Ally, Capital One 360, and Wealthfront are examples to compare—not endorsements. Confirm FDIC coverage, fees, transfer rules, and the current rate before opening one.

2
Calculate the finish line

Essential monthly expenses × 3 is the starter target; × 6 provides more protection. At $3,000 a month, that is $9,000 for three months or $18,000 for six.

3
Automate every payday

Schedule a repeatable transfer from checking—$250 or $500 per paycheck are examples, not requirements. A smaller amount still counts.

4
Redirect after the target is full

Keep the reserve intact, then send future surplus to investing and your debt-paydown plan.

Size your reserve

Use the lower end for stable income and the higher end when income is variable or dependents rely on you.

Sequence step 06 · Long-term investing

Make growth automatic.

Invest the same amount on a schedule instead of guessing the best day. A small automatic contribution can run alongside debt once your cash foundation is secure.

Why this step?
The account is not the investment

A Roth IRA and a taxable brokerage are containers. After money arrives, you still choose what to buy. A Roth IRA offers tax advantages but has eligibility, contribution, and withdrawal rules. A taxable account is more flexible, and eligible holdings may later serve as collateral for a margin loan—but approval is required, interest accrues, falling markets can trigger a margin call or forced sale, and borrowed money can magnify losses.

1
Choose one major brokerage

Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard are examples. Compare fees, recurring purchases, and support.

2
Open the right account containers

Use a Roth IRA first when eligible and within the annual limit; use a taxable brokerage for additional flexible investing.

3
Pick the investments

VOO is broad large-company exposure. QQQ or QQQM follows the more concentrated Nasdaq-100. The framework’s 70/30 or 50/50 mixes are examples, not guarantees or personalized recommendations.

4
Schedule the recurring buy

In the brokerage, look for Transfers or Automatic Investments, link checking, then choose weekly, biweekly, or monthly. $250 or $1,000 per month are examples—use an amount you can sustain. Confirm the cash is invested, not merely transferred.

Sequence step 07 · Home readiness

Know exactly where leverage begins—and where it stacks.

A mortgage lets you control a home with less cash up front — but the home can fall in value while the loan stays put.

Why this step?
Borrowing against leverage, in plain English

Your mortgage is the first layer of leverage. A later HELOC can add a second loan against the same home, while a cash-out refinance generally replaces the original mortgage with a larger new one. A margin loan adds debt against investments whose value can move every day. When borrowed assets support more borrowing, losses, rate changes, and payment pressure can compound instead of staying separate.

1
Measure the first layer

Home price minus down payment equals the starting mortgage. Loan-to-value (LTV) is that mortgage divided by the home price. A smaller down payment means a higher LTV and less room for a price decline.

2
Compare the home with your net worth

Sequence treats a price above 3× positive current net worth as high leverage, 1–3× as meaningful leverage, and 1× or less as lower leverage. These are planning bands—not lender rules or a universal affordability test.

3
Protect cash outside the house

Do not count the down payment as part of the checking buffer or emergency reserve. Add closing costs, moving costs, repairs, and an untouched reserve before deciding what is affordable.

4
Understand the second layer

A HELOC increases debt against the home; combined LTV is the mortgage plus HELOC divided by the home value. A margin loan is separate debt against investments and can be called or liquidated if collateral falls.

5
Stress-test before borrowing again

Model a lower home or portfolio value, a higher interest rate, and a temporary income drop. If any one would force you to sell investments, drain reserves, or miss payments, the second loan is too aggressive.

The home-to-net-worth ratio is only a concentration signal. Monthly payment, income stability, loan terms, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and cash left after closing still decide whether a purchase is workable.

Read: Should you learn day trading?
Sequence’s answer: not as a wealth-building step.The plan does not rely on predicting a stock’s next few minutes. It relies on cash reserves, broad diversified funds, recurring contributions, and time. Day trading is a separate speculative skill with a high failure rate—not the next level after beginner investing.
What the outcome data says

A large study of Taiwan’s day traders found that fewer than 1% predictably earned positive abnormal returns after fees. A talented minority exists, but survivorship bias lets social media show the wins and hide the many people who quit after losses.

The $25,000 rule is changing

The longstanding U.S. pattern-day-trader rule required $25,000 in a margin account after four or more day trades in five business days. FINRA replaced that rule effective June 4, 2026, but firms may transition through October 20, 2027 and may set stricter house rules. Check your broker before trading.

Taxes reduce the headline win

In a taxable account, gains on positions held one year or less are generally short-term capital gains taxed at ordinary-income rates. Frequent trades also create more records, realized gains and losses, and wash-sale complications.

If you insist, earn the right to experiment

Finish the Sequence foundation first. Learn market mechanics and order types, paper-trade long enough to test a written strategy, keep records, and use only money you can fully afford to lose. Never use emergency savings, bill money, a credit card, or borrowed funds.

Sources: Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean day-trader study · FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10 · IRS Topic 409

Read: Find your guilt-free spending number
Sequence’s answer: spend what survives the system.Guilt-free spending is not whatever happens to be sitting in checking. It is the money left after this month’s obligations and the transfers that protect your future have already been handled. If the number is truly available, you can spend it on what matters to you without apologizing to the plan.
Calculate the real number

Start with monthly take-home pay. Subtract essential bills, minimum debt payments, planned checking-buffer and emergency-reserve transfers, employer-match contributions, your automatic long-term investment, and savings for known upcoming costs. What remains is available for flexible spending.

Turn the month into an easy limit

Divide the monthly amount by 4.3 for a practical weekly number. Move it to a separate spending account or track it on one paid-in-full card. When that amount is gone, pause until the next week instead of borrowing from next month.

Why strict budgets often fall apart

Tracking every coffee can create work without improving the decision that matters: whether total flexible spending fits. Use a few useful buckets—needs, future goals, and guilt-free spending—then automate the first two so the third does not require constant judgment.

Spend according to your values

Spend freely inside the limit on the things you genuinely care about, and cut harder on the things you do not. A planned dinner, trip, hobby, or convenience is not a failure. The point is to make trade-offs visible before the purchase, not feel guilty afterward.

Know when spending is not yet guilt-free

Pull back if the purchase requires a carried credit-card balance, skips a required payment, drains the checking buffer or emergency reserve, or cancels an automatic contribution you already committed to. Save for a larger want in advance instead of turning it into a new monthly payment.

Reset when life changes

Recalculate after a raise, rent change, new debt payment, wedding plan, move, or other major shift. A higher income does not automatically create the same amount of new spending room; first decide how much of the change strengthens the sequence.

TAKE-HOME PAY − OBLIGATIONS − PLANNED TRANSFERS − KNOWN UPCOMING COSTS = GUILT-FREE SPENDING
